## Hook
Artificial Super Intelligence is not a near-term threat or a product announcement. It's a theoretical boundary — and the reason it matters now is that thinking about its implications is what drives AI safety and ethics work today.

## Scene 1 — What ASI Describes
ASI refers to a system that surpasses human cognitive capability across every domain simultaneously — not just processing speed, but scientific reasoning, creative problem-solving, social intelligence, and general wisdom. It's not a smarter search engine. It's a system that would outperform the best human minds in every field at once. That's a conceptual threshold, not a specification anyone is currently building to.

## Scene 2 — Why a Theoretical Concept Has Practical Relevance
ASI remains theoretical, but the questions it raises are not. If a system could improve its own intelligence faster than humans could understand or govern its development, what safeguards would need to be in place? Who defines the objectives it optimises for? How do you build alignment between machine goals and human values at that scale? These aren't science fiction thought experiments — they're the foundational questions that AI safety researchers work on today, because establishing those frameworks at the ANI and AGI stage is far easier than trying to retrofit them at ASI.

## Scene 3 — The Ethical and Safety Dimension
The source material on ASI is explicit: its potential realisation prompts important ethical and safety discussions, ensuring that advances in AI capability are matched by advances in societal safeguards. For IT professionals, this context matters because the ethics and governance work in Week 12 of this series traces directly back to this point. The choices made in how AI systems are designed and constrained at the narrow stage have long-range implications. ASI is where those implications arrive at their logical extreme.

## Takeaway
ASI is theoretical today, but it's not irrelevant — it defines the outer boundary of what AI governance needs to account for, which is why safety discussions start now rather than later.
