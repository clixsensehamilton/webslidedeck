## Hook
Knowing that data science needs three pillars is one thing. Understanding what breaks when one is missing — and recognising those failures in the wild — is what makes the framework actually useful.

## Scene 1 — When Business Meets Code (Without the Math)
This combination produces traditional software: deterministic systems built around explicit rules. Applications that follow instructions, enforce logic, and handle exactly the cases their developers anticipated. These systems are reliable and auditable — but they can't adapt. When the real world produces a case the rules didn't cover, the software fails or requires a developer to update it. Most legacy enterprise systems were built this way, and most IT teams know exactly how brittle they get over time.

## Scene 2 — When Business Meets Math (Without the Code)
Strip out the engineering layer and you get traditional research: rigorous analysis, domain-informed models, statistically valid conclusions — that exist as documents. Reports and studies that describe the world accurately but have no mechanism to act on it. The insight is real; the operationalisation is missing. This is the pattern behind every "AI strategy" presentation that never shipped a production system.

## Scene 3 — When Code Meets Math (Without the Domain)
This is the most dangerous pairing for organisations that don't see it coming. Machine learning built without business context can be technically excellent and operationally useless — or worse, harmful. The model trains, the accuracy metrics look good, and then it makes recommendations that don't reflect how the real system works, because nobody with domain knowledge was in the room when the problem was framed. Garbage in, precise garbage out.

## Scene 4 — When All Three Align
Data science is what happens when a team brings domain credibility, engineering rigour, and mathematical capability to the same problem at the same time. Each pillar checks the others: domain knowledge keeps the math honest, engineering makes the math deployable, and the math makes engineering decisions more than guesswork. The output isn't just a model — it's an insight that can be acted on by a real organisation.

## Takeaway
The three-pillar model isn't just a diagram — it's a diagnostic: when an AI project fails, one of these three pillars is usually what's missing.
